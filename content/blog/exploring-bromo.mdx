The alarm went off at 3:00 AM. I had gone to bed at 10 — or tried to — in a small guesthouse in Cemoro Lawang, the last village before the crater rim of Mount Bromo. The mattress was thin, the blanket thinner, and the cold crept in through the walls like it had somewhere to be.

I dressed in layers, grabbed my camera bag, and walked out into the dark.

## The Ride Up

The jeep driver, Pak Slamet, was already waiting outside. He had made this trip a thousand times — you could tell from the way he navigated the switchbacks without headlights, or at least without worrying much about them. The lunar landscape below us was barely visible, just shapes against a slightly lighter dark.

By 4:30 AM we were at the viewpoint. A crowd had already gathered, fifty or sixty people pressed against the railings, all pointed the same direction. I found a small gap and set up my tripod.

![The viewpoint before dawn](https://picsum.photos/seed/bromo-dawn/1200/800)

## Waiting for Light

There is something meditative about waiting for a sunrise you are not sure will happen. A thick bank of cloud sat over the caldera and we could only guess at the mountain behind it. People around me whispered. Someone opened a thermos of coffee.

Then at 5:47 AM, the clouds shifted.

The sun hit the ash plain below — a grey-white desert of volcanic sand, three kilometers across, flat as a table — and turned it gold. Bromo's cone appeared, smoking quietly, its silhouette sharp against the haze. Everyone exhaled at once.

> "You can spend weeks planning a shot, and the light still decides when to show up."

I made about 300 frames in fifteen minutes. Maybe five of them are worth keeping. That ratio felt about right.

## On the Crater Rim

Later in the morning I walked up to the crater itself. The path is a combination of sand, stone steps, and steel handrails bolted into the rock. The sulfur smell hits you about halfway up — sharp and chemical, like struck matches.

The crater is maybe 700 meters wide and opens straight down into nothing visible. You hear it before you see it: a low continuous roar, wind through a geological throat.

I stayed up there for an hour. The light changed four times.

## What I Brought Back

Three photos I'm proud of. A persistent cough from the sulfur. A new rule I've added to myself: **bring more layers than you think you need, and fewer expectations about the shot**.

The best images from that trip weren't the dramatic sunrise frames — they were the quiet ones. Pak Slamet eating breakfast in his jeep. A tourist's scarf caught in the wind. A dog sleeping on warm ash.

I keep coming back to those.
